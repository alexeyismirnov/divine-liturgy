Nicene Creed

People:

I believe in one God, the Father almighty, Maker of heaven and earth, and of all things visible and invisible.

And in one Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, the only-begotten, begotten of the Father before all ages. Light of Light; true God of true God; begotten, not made; of one essence with the Father, by whom all things were made; who for us men and for our salvation came down from heaven, and was incarnate of the Holy Spirit of the Virgin Mary, and became man. And He was crucified for us under Pontius Pilate, and suffered, and was buried. And the third day He rose again, according to the Scriptures, and ascended into heaven, and sits at the right hand of the Father; and He shall come again with glory to judge the living and the dead; whose Kingdom shall have no end.

And in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the Giver of Life, who proceeds from the Father; who with the Father and the Son together is worshiped and glorified; who spoke by the prophets.

In one Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic Church. I acknowledge one baptism for the remission of sins. I look for the resurrection of the dead. And the life of the world to come. Amen.

The deacon exclaims: ▶

The curtains are shut.

The priest lifts the aer and holds it over the Holy Gifts. If several priests are serving, then they lift the holy aer and hold it over the Holy Gifts and wave it saying to themselves the Confession of the Faith, as do the people.

Story From Church History

St Polycarp (69–155) was a disciple of St John the apostle and the bishop of Smyrna (on the west coast of what is now Turkey). During his lifetime, a controversy arose concerning the timing of Easter. Polycarp and the churches of Asia Minor celebrated Easter based on when the Jewish Passover occurred (on the 14th day of the Jewish month of Nisan) regardless of the day of the week on which it fell. Most other Christians remembered Christ’s death on Friday and His resurrection on Sunday.

Toward the end of his long life, Polycarp made a difficult trip to Rome and discussed the problem with Anicetus, the bishop of Rome. The two bishops discussed the issue at length, but neither one was able to convince the other. Polycarp said that he had personally learned this practice from St John while Anicetus said that he was following the practice of those who came before him in Rome. Instead of breaking fellowship with one another over the issue (as some did 50 years later), the two men decided that their unity and the unity of the church were more important than the timing of the Easter celebration. They agreed to disagree. As a sign of their unbroken fellowship, Anicetus gave Polycarp the honor of officiating a Eucharist at the Roman church where they assuredly exchanged the kiss of peace. Eusebius (340) states in his history of the Church that “they parted from each other in peace…maintaining the peace of the whole church.”
