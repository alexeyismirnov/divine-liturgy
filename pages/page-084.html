The Rite of Warmth (Zeon)16

After placing the Holy Bread into the Chalice, a little warm water is added. This rite is particular to the Byzantine Liturgy and is found in no other liturgical tradition. From the 11th century on, Byzantine commentators explained that the use of hot water was to ‘show that we receive in communion not the cold body and blood of a cadaver, but the warm and life-giving Body and Blood of our Redeemer.’

We receive in communion the warm and life-giving Body and Blood of our Redeemer.

Nicholas Cabasilas (ca. 1350) offered one explanation of the rite: The priest drops into the chalice a little warm water to symbolize the descent of the Holy Spirit upon the Church. For the Holy Spirit is given when the whole plan of redemption is completed. And now the descent of the Spirit comes about when the sacrifice has been offered and the holy offerings have reached their perfection.…. What is the effect and the result of the sufferings and works and teaching of Christ? Considered in relation to ourselves, it is nothing other than the descent of the Holy Spirit upon the Church.

So it is fitting that this should be represented after the other mysteries, as it is when the warm water is poured into the chalice. Since this warm water is not only water, but shares the nature of fire, it signifies the Holy Spirit, who is sometimes represented by water, and who came down upon the Apostles in the form of fire. …for the mysteries also represent the Church, which is the Body of Christ; she received the Holy Spirit after our Lord’s ascension; now she receives the gift of the Holy Spirit after the offerings have been accepted at the heavenly altar. God, who has accepted them, sends us the Holy Spirit in return, as we have said; for then and now there is one Mediator and one Spirit.

  1. The history of the zeon rite is very complex and historical texts do not make it clear at what date the Byzantines began adding hot water to the already-consecrated chalice immediately before communion. It is not until the 11th century that symbolic explanations of the zeon rite appear. Robert Taft has suggested that from the very beginning the Byzantines, like most Christian communities, added water to the wine before the consecration as is done today during the rite of the Prothesis. Where it was customary in domestic usage, as it was in most cultures, hot water was used.

    “The desire to keep the chalice warm right up until communion to signify that we receive the body and Blood of the living, Risen Christ, led to delaying the infusion of hot water until before the Great Entrance and ultimately until just before communion, or if hot water had been added at the henosis [that is during the Prothesis], to adding additional hot water to the chalice just before communion.” Taft, Precommunion, 502.
