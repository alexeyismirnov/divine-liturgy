History of the Great Entrance5

The Great Entrance is simply a development of the original transfer of the peoples’ gifts to the altar. In the early Church, the faithful brought gifts (prosphora) to the Eucharist gathering as a sign of sacrificially giving themselves to God in Christ. Upon entering the meeting place they gave their gifts to the deacon stationed by the entrance. All types of gifts were given, but of greatest importance to the liturgical service were the gifts of bread and wine. From these gifts the deacons selected as much as was needed for communion and when the time came for the eucharistic prayer the bread and wine were brought forward and placed on the table. In time, for various reasons both cultural and religious, this part of the liturgy became enriched…by the addition of an antiphon [the Cherubim Hymn and possibly Psalm 24.7-10], prayers, and other ceremonies. In the great churches, processions could include dozens of participants, including deacons carrying censers, rhipidia*, candles, the aer, and wash basins.

  1. In the early Church, the transfer of gifts was done without any particular ceremony. St Justin the Martyr writing about 150 A.D. explains simply that “after finishing the prayers [of the faithful], we greet one another with a kiss. Then bread and a cup of water and mixed wine are brought to the one presiding over the brethren. He takes it, gives praise and glory to the Father of all in the name of the Son and Holy Ghost, and gives thanks at length for the gifts that we were worthy to receive from Him.”

    As congregations grew and churches were built, separate facilities were created to receive the peoples’ offerings and to prepare the bread and wine for the Eucharist. The gifts were accepted and kept in the diaconicon (also known as the skeuophylakion or sacristy), where the deacons also kept archives, a library, vestments, and the church treasure. Local custom determined the location of the diaconicon: whether inside the church or attached to the narthex or in a separate structure. At Hagia Sophia it was located in a separate building at the north east corner of the main church. The procession taking the Gifts and everything necessary for the Eucharist from the skeuophylakion through the midst of the people in the nave and on to the sanctuary and altar became known as the Great Entrance.
