The Litany Before the Lord’s Prayer

The litany of supplication is each person’s examination of the state of his own soul, asking for forgiveness and for everything necessary in order to live his life to the end in unity of the faith and the communion of the Holy Spirit….

“Pardon and remission of our sins and transgressions, let us ask of the Lord.” This prayer shows us that Holy Communion is received for the “remission of sins” and “forgiveness of transgressions.”

We go to Communion not perfect and sinless, but precisely because we are sinful and imperfect.

“Thus we go to Communion not perfect and sinless, but precisely because we are sinful and imperfect. But we pray that God will make us worthy of partaking of the holy meal “with a pure conscience.” Even though only God can make us sinners worthy, it remains our task to see that our conscience is pure when we partake of the Holy gifts, and it can be pure only when we feel sincere repentance and a desire to do better.”4

The Prayer of Preparation

“This prayer stresses, on the one hand, that communion of the Body and Blood of Christ is the goal of our whole life and hope, and, on the other hand, expresses the fear that we may receive it unworthily and thus for condemnation. We pray that through communion, we may have Christ dwelling in our hearts and may become the Temple of Thy Holy Spirit. This prayer summarizes the whole Liturgy, places us once more before all the aspects of the Mystery, yet this time with an emphasis on the personal character of its reception, the responsibility it puts on those who receive it…. The Church has always stressed the importance of an individual preparation for Communion, the importance for each communicant to evaluate himself and all his life as he approaches the Sacrament. The preparation should not be neglected; of this we are reminded in the prayer of preparation.”5

The History of the Lord’s Prayer in the Liturgy

One of the first references6 to the Lord’s Prayer in the liturgy at Constantinople is the famous sermon of St John Chrysostom given in July 399 concerning the fallen Eutropius. Eutropius was born a slave, but through strange circumstances became the Consul of Emperor Arcadius. By intrigue and deception, and by surrounding the emperor with worldly pleasures, Eutropius became the real power behind the throne and perpetrated all sorts of evil. He eliminated all of his competitors and even cut off their means of escape by abolishing the Church’s right to grant asylum to fugitives – much to the dismay of Archbishop John Chrysostom. Eutropius’ fortunes dramatically reversed one day and with imperial soldiers in pursuit, he fled to the church, drew aside the altar curtains and clung to one of its columns. From there he begged Chrysostom for asylum.

St Augustine (†430) explained in his Sermo Denis 6,3, “Why is it [the Lord’s Prayer] recited before receiving the Body and Blood of Christ? Because human frailty is such that perhaps we entertained some improper thought, something came from our tongue that should not have, the eye glanced at something improper, if the ears heard some enticement they should not have, if perhaps some things have happened as a result of this world’s temptation and the weakness of human life, it is wiped clean by the Lord’s Prayer, where it is said ‘Forgive us our trespasses;’ so that we might approach [the Eucharist] safely, lest we eat and drink what we receive unto our judgment.”

The next day was Sunday and people flooded into the church to hear what was happening. As Chrysostom began his sermon the curtain of the sanctuary was drawn aside revealing “the cowering form of the miserable Eutropius clinging to one of the columns of the Holy Table. Many a time had the Archbishop preached to unheeding ears on the vain and fleeting character of worldly honor, prosperity, luxury, and wealth: now he would force attention, and drive home his lesson to the hearts of his vast congregation by pointing to a visible example of fallen grandeur in the poor wretch who lay groveling behind him.”7

As well as speaking against the vanity of worldly riches and honor, Chrysostom also asked not only Emperor Arcadius but the people to forgive Eutropius by reminding them that they would soon be reciting the Lord’s Prayer as part of the Liturgy. He said, “Moreover let me add some arguments which concern ourselves. For what pardon could you deserve, if the Emperor bears no resentment when he has been insulted, but you, who have experienced nothing of this kind, display so much wrath? How after this assembly has been dissolved will you handle the Holy Mysteries [receive Com—

munion], and repeat that prayer by which we are commanded to say ‘forgive us as we also forgive our debtors’ when you are demanding vengeance upon your debtor? Has he inflicted great wrongs and insults on you? I will not deny it. Yet this is the season not for judgment but for mercy; not for requiring an account, but for showing lovingkindness; not for investigating claims but for conceding them; not for verdicts and vengeance, but for mercy and favor.”8

The Lord’s Prayer

The Lord’s Prayer is the quintessential prayer of all Christianity, … It is the prayer from which all the other prayers of the Church are derived, either directly or indirectly, and which in itself contains the essence of the message of the faith and prescription for salvation….

Throughout the gospels Jesus Christ frequently…refers to the supra-heavenly God, God the Father, as ‘My Father’. Always, in referring to the Father, this familiar ‘My’ is used, thus identifying Himself in a fully filial relationship to the Godhead. No one anywhere else in Holy Scripture takes such liberty.

Yet in the Lord’s Prayer, He extends to us fallen creatures the incomparable and unique privilege of addressing the divine Majesty of the Lord of all, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, the Creator and Ruler of cosmos, He who is and ever shall be, as ‘Father’…. The Greek word is epouranion, supra-heavenly, to remind us that God is not of this world, nor of its families. He transcends all that we know and can conceive, and that we can approach Him only through His only-begotten Son alone. Note the words of the Liturgy which immediately precede the prayer: ‘And make us worthy O master, that with boldness and without condemnation we may dare to call on Thee, the heavenly God, as Father, and to say:’

That we may dare! Dare to call the Lord of all ‘Daddy’ even as we would address our own parent! Note that in the prayer, Jesus tells us to address God as ‘Our Father.’ We are never told to address Him as ‘my Father’. That degree of possessiveness is reserved entirely to the Son of God – Jesus Christ – who is His Son from all eternity by nature. We as human beings may yet become what Christ is by nature through the operation of divine grace. Thus in that sense we, too, may become children of God therefore brothers and sisters of Christ, as it were, by adoption through His infinite mercy and infinite love in the Holy Spirit. And therefore since we belong one to another, all partake equally and uniformly of our human nature. We do so collectively and never as individuals; we must address the living God as “Our Father’ and never in an individual or possessive way.”9

The Deacon Crossing His Orar

While the priest reads the prayer, the deacon stands before the Holy Doors and crosses his orar around himself. This is done so that during the time of communion the deacon can serve without interference. It is done also to demonstrate the deacon’s reverence toward the Holy Mysteries by approaching communion in a way imitating the seraphim covering themselves with their wings. Concerning this, Symeon the New Theologian (†1022) explains: ‘The deacon is adorned with this (orar) as with wings and covers himself in reverence and humility especially during communion, thus imitating the seraphim who, as it is written, have six wings, two of which cover their face, two the feet, and with two they fly, exclaiming, ‘Holy, Holy, Holy.’’11

Bowing the Head (Inclination)

The priest has just reminded the faithful “of their nobility, calling God their Father; now he calls upon them to acknowledge Him as Lord, and to give Him a sign of their servitude by bowing their heads, thus indicating their dependence on Him. They bow before Him not simply as creatures before their Lord and Creator, but as purchased slaves to Him who obtained them at the price of the Blood of His only Son. He possesses us by double right, as slaves whom He has made His children. For His precious Blood both increased our slavery and brought about the Divine adoption.

The Elevation

“The priest takes in his hands the Divine Bread and lifts it up saying ‘The Holy things are for the holy!’ This ancient rite of Elevation is the original form of communion. In its perfect conciseness it expresses the antinomy*, the supra-rational nature of religion. It forbids anyone who is not holy to partake of the Divine Holiness. But none is holy, but the Holy One. And the choir answers, affirming it: One is Holy. One is the Lord Jesus Christ. Yet, come and receive, for He has sanctified us with His Holiness, has made us His holy people. Again and again the mystery of the Eucharist is revealed as the mystery of the Church, the Body of Christ in which we eternally become what we are called to be.”13

Nicholas Cabasilas (ca. 1350) explains ‘for no one has holiness of himself; it is not the consequence of human virtue, but comes to all from Him and through Him. It is as if we were to place mirrors beneath the sun; each would shine, and send forth rays of light, so that one would think in truth there is but one sun which shines in all; just so Christ, the only Holy One, shines upon the faithful, shines in so many souls, and gives light to many saints; to the glory of the Father.’14

No one has holiness of himself; it is not the consequence of human virtue, but comes to all from Him and through Him.

The Manual Acts of Preparation for Communion

Breaking the Bread (Fraction)

The early Church called the whole eucharistic service “breaking of bread,” for this rite was central in the liturgical celebration. Its meaning is clear: the same and one bread which is given to many, is the same and one Christ becoming the life of many, uniting them in Himself: Unite all of us to one another who become partakers of the one Bread and Cup in the Communion of the Holy Spirit …(the Liturgy of St Basil).

Then the priest breaking the Bread says: Divided and distributed is the Lamb of God: who is divided, yet not disunited; who is ever eaten, yet never consumed; but sanctifying those who partake thereof. This is the one source of life that brings all to it and redeems the unity of all men under one Head – Christ.15

“Even though every day the immaculate Body of Christ is broken and His Blood is poured out on the Holy Altar, still the smallest particle of the Lamb is the whole Christ. And even though many liturgies are performed at the same moment around the world, still there are not many bodies of Jesus Christ, since in each the same Christ is present. So when someone communes in the smallest part of the Bread, he communes in His whole Body and Blood.

Commingling

The commingling rite, i.e. placing the Holy Bread into the Chalice, is an ancient part of the Divine Liturgy.17 Theodore of Mopsuestia (ca. 392) writes: ‘And he [the bishop] joins them together reuniting the Body and Blood, showing everyone that, although two, they are one in power and are not separate after the separation which occurred at the moment of the death and passion undergone by the Body of our Lord when He was crucified on the cross for all of us. …for this reason it is customary to drop the Body into the Chalice little by little, in order to show that they are inseparable and one in power and confer the same grace to those who receive them.’

“The commingling ‘completes’ the mystery by perfecting the union of the elements as a sign of the resurrection and consequently the union of Body, Blood, and Spirit in the incorruptible Risen Lord, so that they may return united to us in communion. Though the reality was already accomplished at the Epiclesis [the invocation of the Holy Spirit], it must also be so in sign at the completion of the sacrifice, before being broken and distributed in communion.”19

“Whereas the consecrated Bread and Wine is a twofold sign of Christ’s sacrificial Body and Blood, the commingling, symbolizing the union of these two species, images the glorified humanity of the Risen Christ and our participation, via communion, in His risen life.”20

The Rite of Warmth (Zeon)21

After placing the Holy Bread into the Chalice, a little warm water is added. This rite is particular to the Byzantine Liturgy and is found in no other liturgical tradition. From the 11th century on, Byzantine commentators explained that the use of hot water was to ‘show that we receive in communion not the cold body and blood of a cadaver, but the warm and life-giving Body and Blood of our Redeemer.’

We receive in communion the warm and life-giving Body and Blood of our Redeemer.

Nicholas Cabasilas (ca. 1350) offered one explanation of the rite: ‘The priest drops into the chalice a little warm water to symbolize the descent of the Holy Spirit upon the Church. For the Holy Spirit is given when the whole plan of redemption is completed. And now the descent of the Spirit comes about when the sacrifice has been offered and the holy offerings have reached their perfection.…. What is the effect and the result of the sufferings and works and teaching of Christ? Considered in relation to ourselves, it is nothing other than the descent of the Holy Spirit upon the Church.

So it is fitting that this should be represented after the other mysteries, as it is when the warm water is poured into the chalice. Since this warm water is not only water, but shares the nature of fire, it signifies the Holy Spirit, who is sometimes represented by water, and who came down upon the Apostles in the form of fire. …for the mysteries also represent the Church, which is the Body of Christ; she received the Holy Spirit after our Lord’s ascension; now she receives the gift of the Holy Spirit after the offerings have been accepted at the heavenly altar. God, who has accepted them, sends us the Holy Spirit in return, as we have said; for then and now there is one Mediator and one Spirit.’23
