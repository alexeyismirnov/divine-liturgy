The History of the Lord’s Prayer in the Liturgy

One of the first references to the Lord’s Prayer in the liturgy at Constantinople is the famous sermon of St John Chrysostom given in July 399 concerning the fallen Eutropius. Eutropius was born a slave, but through strange circumstances became the Consul of Emperor Arcadius. By intrigue and deception, and by surrounding the emperor with worldly pleasures, Eutropius became the real power behind the throne and perpetrated all sorts of evil. He eliminated all of his competitors and even cut off their means of escape by abolishing the Church’s right to grant asylum to fugitives – much to the dismay of Archbishop John Chrysostom. Eutropius’ fortunes dramatically reversed one day and with imperial soldiers in pursuit, he fled to the church, drew aside the altar curtains and clung to one of its columns. From there he begged Chrysostom for asylum.

St Augustine (†430) explained in his Sermo Denis 6,3, “Why is it [the Lord’s Prayer] recited before receiving the Body and Blood of Christ? Because human frailty is such that perhaps we entertained some improper thought, something came from our tongue that should not have, the eye glanced at something improper, if the ears heard some enticement they should not have, if perhaps some things have happened as a result of this world’s temptation and the weakness of human life, it is wiped clean by the Lord’s Prayer, where it is said ‘Forgive us our trespasses;’ so that we might approach [the Eucharist] safely, lest we eat and drink what we receive unto our judgment.”

The next day was Sunday and people flooded into the church to hear what was happening. As Chrysostom began his sermon the curtain of the sanctuary was drawn aside revealing the cowering form of the miserable Eutropius clinging to one of the columns of the Holy Table. Many a time had the Archbishop preached to unheeding ears on the vain and fleeting character of worldly honor, prosperity, luxury, and wealth: now he would force attention, and drive home his lesson to the hearts of his vast congregation by pointing to a visible example of fallen grandeur in the poor wretch who lay groveling behind him.

As well as speaking against the vanity of worldly riches and honor, Chrysostom also asked not only Emperor Arcadius but the people to forgive Eutropius by reminding them that they would soon be reciting the Lord’s Prayer as part of the Liturgy. He said, “Moreover let me add some arguments which concern ourselves. For what pardon could you deserve, if the Emperor bears no resentment when he has been insulted, but you, who have experienced nothing of this kind, display so much wrath?
