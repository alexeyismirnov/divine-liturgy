Let us lift up our hearts!

“Commenting on this part of the Divine Liturgy around 1350 A.D., Nicolas Cabasilas states: “Having dignified us with such a prayer and thus raised our souls up from the earth, the priest raises our thoughts also, saying: ‘Let us lift up our hearts!’ – let us be heavenly minded, not earthly-minded. The faithful give their consent, and say that their hearts are where their treasure is – there where Christ is, who sits at the right hand of the Father.’ ‘We lift them up unto the Lord.’2”

Liturgical Fans (Rhipidia)

In the early Church, fans made of fine cloth or peacock’s feathers were waved over the Holy Bread and Chalice to keep them from being defiled by flies. Now they are generally made of metal in the shape of a disk and are decorated with six winged seraphim.3

“They show by this ritual the greatness of the Body lying there.”

An angel holding a rhipidia

The Eucharistic Prayer was originally ‘the’ prayer, the only prayer in the whole rite, and it was there that the whole meaning of the rite had to be stated, if it was to be put into words at all in the course of the service.”4 From the first century, the eucharistic prayer has been one of the four foundational “actions” of the communion service. These four actions are:

(1) THE OFFERTORY; bread and wine are ‘taken’ and placed on the table.

(2) THE EUCHARISTIC PRAYER; the one presiding gives thanks to God over bread and wine.

“I am the living bread which came down from heaven. If anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever”

St John 6.51

(3) THE FRACTION; the bread is broken.

(4) COMMUNION; the bread and wine are distributed.

All these actions were derived from the traditional Jewish meal which Christ shared with His disciples at the Last Supper. The prayer, our eucharistic “thanksgiving” prayer, developed from the prayer of blessing and thanksgiving which Jesus prayed and it always was recited at Jewish meals. “…the Lord Jesus in the night in which He was betrayed took bread; and when He had given thanks, He broke it…” (1 Cor. 11.23-24) “This was a long prayer said by the host or father of the family in the name of all who had eaten the meal. It was a strict obligation on all male Jews after any food ‘not less than the size of an olive’ or ‘of an egg’.”5 The modern eucharistic prayer is made of four parts: The Preface, Remembrance, Epiclesis, and Intercession.

What originated as the Jewish giving of thanks to God for the food which gives mortal life, became the Church’s giving of thanks for the Food which gives immortal Life – the Bread of Life - Christ Jesus Himself.
