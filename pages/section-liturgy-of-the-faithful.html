The Offertory

The Offertory is the sacrificial act of the Church offering to God the oblation of our lives.

The Cherubim Hymn, the censing of the Altar and congregation, and the transfer of the eucharistic elements to the Altar (Great Entrance) constitute the first essential ‘movement’ of the Eucharist: the Offertory, which is the sacrificial act of the Church offering to God the oblation of our lives. We speak very often of the sacrifice of Christ, but so easily forget that the sacrifice of Christ requests and implies our own sacrifice, or rather our ‘uniting ourselves’ to the sacrifice of Christ, for we are His Body and partakers of His Life. A sacrifice is the natural movement of love, which is the gift of oneself, a self-denial for the sake of the Other. When I love somebody, my life is in him whom I love; I give him my life – freely, willingly, joyfully – and this ‘giving away’ becomes the very meaning of my life.

Prayers of the Faithful

As the first part of the Liturgy of the Word begins with a call to the believers to pray in peace to the Lord, so the Liturgy of the Faithful also calls for those present to pray in peace to which the choir responds ‘Lord have mercy.’ During this time, the priest recites the First Prayer of the Faithful in which he prays for forgiveness of his own sins and of the peoples’ and also prays for help in offering the bloodless sacrifice. The deacon then begins a second prayer during which time the priest says the Second Prayer of the Faithful where he prays for the cleansing of the bodies and souls of those who stand before the Altar, for progress in life, faith, and spiritual understanding of those who pray, and that the taking of communion might be done worthily…. At the end of both these prayers the deacon proclaims ‘Wisdom’ which reminds those present that their attention during the offering of the gifts for sacrifice on the Altar must be focused and they should not be distracted since the consecration of the gifts is done with particular care.

The Aer:

The large covering cloth is called the aer, (or air) since the priest and deacon wave it above the Holy Gifts moving the air and because it covers the Holy Gifts as the air covers the earth. This waving of the aer represents the overshadowing of the Holy Gifts by the Holy Spirit. The aer placed on the gifts represents the grave of the Savior and symbolizes the closing of the tomb. Because of this, the aer is decorated with scenes of the taking down of the Body of Christ from the cross and His placement in the tomb.

The Offertory (continued)

Thus in the procession of Offertory our very life is being brought to the Altar, presented to God in an act of love and adoration. True it is the ‘King of Kings and Lord of Lords who comes to be sacrificed’ (hymn of Offertory, Great Saturday), it is His Entrance as Priest and Oblation*; and yet, in Him and with Him, we also lay on the paten*, the members of His Body, the partakers of His Humanity. ‘Let us put aside all earthly cares,’ sings the choir; and, indeed, have not all our cares been assumed in this unique and ultimate Care that transfigures all our life – in this movement of love which brings us to the Source and the Giver and the Content of Life?

The Cherubim Hymn4

This hymn calls those present Christians, mystically representing the Cherubim (that is, comparing in some mystical way to the Cherubim and singing together with them the thrice-holy hymn to the Life-Creating Trinity) to lay aside all worldly cares. In other words, not thinking about property, glory, or pleasure but to worthily receive the King of all earthly and heavenly creatures with the honor paid to Him by the heavenly angelic hosts singing ‘Alleluia!’

“The Cherubim Hymn reminds us that from this moment until the end of the Liturgy we must be vigilant and must lay aside all earthly cares since, during communion, all will receive Christ, the Great King.”

St Germanus (†A.D. 730)

This hymn began to be used in church services during the time of Emperor Justinian II (565-578). It was introduced into the Liturgy in order that during the time church officials brought the Gifts to the Altar from where they had been prepared, the faithful, by listening to this hymn, would be filled with the most holy thoughts and would be prepared to worthily receive the King of all.

History of the Great Entrance5

The Great Entrance is simply a development of the original transfer of the peoples’ gifts to the altar. In the early Church, the faithful brought gifts (prosphora) to the Eucharist gathering as a sign of sacrificially giving themselves to God in Christ. Upon entering the meeting place they gave their gifts to the deacon stationed by the entrance. All types of gifts were given, but of greatest importance to the liturgical service were the gifts of bread and wine. From these gifts the deacons selected as much as was needed for communion and when the time came for the eucharistic prayer the bread and wine were brought forward and placed on the table. In time, for various reasons both cultural and religious, this part of the liturgy became enriched…by the addition of an antiphon [the Cherubim Hymn and possibly Psalm 24.7-10], prayers, and other ceremonies. In the great churches, processions could include dozens of participants, including deacons carrying censers, rhipidia*, candles, the aer, and wash basins.

  1. In the early Church, the transfer of gifts was done without any particular ceremony. St Justin the Martyr writing about 150 A.D. explains simply that “after finishing the prayers [of the faithful], we greet one another with a kiss. Then bread and a cup of water and mixed wine are brought to the one presiding over the brethren. He takes it, gives praise and glory to the Father of all in the name of the Son and Holy Ghost, and gives thanks at length for the gifts that we were worthy to receive from Him.”

    As congregations grew and churches were built, separate facilities were created to receive the peoples’ offerings and to prepare the bread and wine for the Eucharist. The gifts were accepted and kept in the diaconicon (also known as the skeuophylakion or sacristy), where the deacons also kept archives, a library, vestments, and the church treasure. Local custom determined the location of the diaconicon: whether inside the church or attached to the narthex or in a separate structure. At Hagia Sophia it was located in a separate building at the north east corner of the main church. The procession taking the Gifts and everything necessary for the Eucharist from the skeuophylakion through the midst of the people in the nave and on to the sanctuary and altar became known as the Great Entrance.

The Great Entrance

It is always the whole Church—the unity of faith and love—that offers and is being offered

“May the Lord God remember all of you in His Kingdom…” says the priest while approaching the Altar with the Gifts. The love, which Christ has ‘poured into our hearts’ (Romans 5.5) has its natural expression in the mutual love of Christians for each other. The Kingdom of God is that of perfect unity – ‘that they may be one, even as we are one’ (John 17.11). Therefore, there is no other way to approach God but in love. He remembers us if we remember each other. Whatever the number of those who offer this Eucharist, it is always the whole Church – the unity of faith and love – that offers and is being offered, and this organic unity of the Church is expressed in the commemoration at the Great Entrance.

The Cherubic Hymn was originally chanted as one complete hymn, possibly as the antiphonal refrain to Psalm 24.7-10. During the Great Entrance, while the hymn was being sung, believers wanted to be remembered by God. In some cultures they would even fall down before the procession, forcing the ministers to step over them. This desire to be remembered at this particularly solemn point in the Liturgy led to dividing the Hymn into two parts so that between the two parts the deacon and the priest could pray out loud for the whole Church during the Great Entrance. Gradually, the practice arose of commemorating aloud the emperor and the patriarch if they were present at the liturgy. These commemorations were gradually multiplied to include other orders in the Church, but initially they were done silently unless those in question were actually present.

Symbolic Meaning of the Great Entrance

In the earliest celebration of the Eucharist, the transfer of the peoples’ gifts to the Altar was done without any particular ceremony. Upon arrival at the meeting, believers would give the deacon their gifts of bread and wine and at some point during the service the deacon would take these offerings and place them on the Altar. Between the fourth and the seventh centuries many parts of the Divine Liturgy were made more ceremonial, including this transfer of the gifts, which became known as the Great Entrance. During the latter part of this era, a new layer of ‘symbolic’ meaning

was also added to the whole Liturgy. As Taft explains: St Germanus (†A.D. 730) integrated another level of interpretation into Byzantine liturgical understanding; one that was equally rooted in the New Testament and also found, though far less prominently, in Maximus (†A.D. 630) and other earlier Byzantine liturgical writings. This was the interpretation of the Eucharist, not only as the anamnesis* [recalling] of, but also as the actual figure of salvation history in Jesus. Each part of the service was linked with particular events in the life, death, burial, and resurrection of Christ.

The transfer of the gifts from the Prothesis to the Holy Altar and the singing of the Cherubim Hymn are symbolic of Jesus Christ’s journey from Bethany to Jerusalem to endure His suffering and death. When He entered Jerusalem there were many people, including children, who offered songs in a visible way to Jesus as king and as one who triumphs over death. In a similar but spiritual way, the angels continually offer songs to the thrice-Holy Trinity and people now lift up the Cherubim Hymn to God.

The placing of the Holy Gifts on the Altar represents the crucifixion of Christ, taking down His body from the cross, and His burial. This is seen in the prayers (troparion*) which are said when the Gifts are placed on the Holy Table. The Diskos* [paten] symbolizes the hands of Joseph and Nicodemus who took down the Immaculate Body of Christ from the cross, wrapped Him in clean burial cloths, and placed Him in the new tomb. The Chalice symbolizes the cup with the blood which flowed from Christ’s side, hands, and feet. The covering on the Diskos represents the cloth which was placed around His head, the covering on the Chalice represents the cloth in which His body was wrapped, and the aer represents the stone which Joseph placed on the entrance to the tomb. The incense represents the spices which were placed on the body of the Savior; and the Holy Altar and the Antimension represent the grave itself. Finally, the closed Holy Doors and the drawn curtain represent the door to the tomb which was securely closed by the soldiers and the seal. The Chalice is placed to the right of the diskos since the blood and water flowed from the right rib of the Savior.

Prayer of the Prothesis

During this litany the priest prays: ‘O Lord God Almighty…’ This prayer is called the Prayer of Offering (or Prothesis) since the offering is repeated several times during the liturgy, three times in fact. During the first offering – at the Prothesis – the priest prays that the Lord God would bless the gifts which are offered…. During the second offering – which is done now – the priest prays that the Lord would enable him to offer the gifts and spiritual sacrifices to Him, make us worthy to find grace before Him, that our sacrifice would be acceptable, and that the good Spirit of God’s grace would rest on the clergy, on the gifts which have been placed on the altar and on the people. These two offerings are only preparations for sacrifice…. With the third offering, which is the greatest and fullest, after remembering Christ’s suffering and counting God’s good works, the priest proclaims, ‘Thine own of Thine own we offer unto Thee in behalf of all and for all,’ after which comes the actual sanctification of the Divine Gifts.

Litany of Supplication

The “Angel of Peace” litany, or the αἰτήσεις (asking), is an ancient prayer said at various times in a variety of church services. In the Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380 A.D.) it is used as a concluding litany before the dismissal of the faithful at matins and vespers. St John Chrysostom refers to the prayer in his Homily 3 on the epistle to the Colossians: “For each believer has an Angel; since even from the beginning, every one of those that were approved had his Angel…. If then we have Angels, let us be sober, as though we were in the presence of tutors; for there is a demon present also. Therefore we pray, asking for the Angel of peace, and everywhere we ask for peace (for there is nothing equal to this); peace.”

Story From Church History

St Polycarp (69–155) was a disciple of St John the apostle and the bishop of Smyrna (on the west coast of what is now Turkey). During his lifetime, a controversy arose concerning the timing of Easter. Polycarp and the churches of Asia Minor celebrated Easter based on when the Jewish Passover occurred (on the 14th day of the Jewish month of Nisan) regardless of the day of the week on which it fell. Most other Christians remembered Christ’s death on Friday and His resurrection on Sunday.

Toward the end of his long life, Polycarp made a difficult trip to Rome and discussed the problem with Anicetus, the bishop of Rome. The two bishops discussed the issue at length, but neither one was able to convince the other. Polycarp said that he had personally learned this practice from St John while Anicetus said that he was following the practice of those who came before him in Rome. Instead of breaking fellowship with one another over the issue (as some did 50 years later), the two men decided that their unity and the unity of the church were more important than the timing of the Easter celebration. They agreed to disagree. As a sign of their unbroken fellowship, Anicetus gave Polycarp the honor of officiating a Eucharist at the Roman church where they assuredly exchanged the kiss of peace. Eusebius (340) states in his history of the Church that “they parted from each other in peace…maintaining the peace of the whole church.”

The Doors!

After expressing our unity of love, we express, in the Creed, our unity of faith.

In the ancient church, after dismissing the catechumens, the door keepers were told to stand by the men’s door, and the deaconesses by the women’s door. They were to keep the believers inside the church during the bloodless sacrifice and prevent all others from entering… The words ‘The doors, the doors!’ were intended for those people who were responsible for keeping the doors closed during the Liturgy…. When, in later times, the old discipline was relaxed and entrance into the church was not guarded with the original attention and strictness, the exclamation, ‘The doors, the doors!’ was not maintained simply as a reminder of old practices, but rather as a reminder for the believers to examine themselves to see if they were qualified to remain in the church…and to show that they were not only believers in name.

In the early Church, fans made of fine cloth or peacock’s feathers were waved over the Holy Bread and Chalice to keep them from being defiled by flies. Now they are generally made of metal in the shape of a disk and are decorated with six winged seraphim.

The Eucharistic Prayer was originally ‘the’ prayer, the only prayer in the whole rite, and it was there that the whole meaning of the rite had to be stated, if it was to be put into words at all in the course of the service. From the first century, the eucharistic prayer has been one of the four foundational “actions” of the communion service. These four actions are:

(1) THE OFFERTORY; bread and wine are ‘taken’ and placed on the table.

(2) THE EUCHARISTIC PRAYER; the one presiding gives thanks to God over bread and wine.

“I am the living bread which came down from heaven. If anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever”

(3) THE FRACTION; the bread is broken.

(4) COMMUNION; the bread and wine are distributed.

All these actions were derived from the traditional Jewish meal which Christ shared with His disciples at the Last Supper. The prayer, our eucharistic “thanksgiving” prayer, developed from the prayer of blessing and thanksgiving which Jesus prayed and it always was recited at Jewish meals. “…the Lord Jesus in the night in which He was betrayed took bread; and when He had given thanks, He broke it…” (1 Cor. 11.23-24) This was a long prayer said by the host or father of the family in the name of all who had eaten the meal. It was a strict obligation on all male Jews after any food ‘not less than the size of an olive’ or ‘of an egg’. The modern eucharistic prayer is made of four parts: The Preface, Remembrance, Epiclesis, and Intercession.

What originated as the Jewish giving of thanks to God for the food which gives mortal life, became the Church’s giving of thanks for the Food which gives immortal Life – the Bread of Life - Christ Jesus Himself.

The transformation of the elements into the Body and the Blood of Christ takes place on the heavenly altar, in the Kingdom of God, which is beyond time and the ‘laws’ of ‘this world.’ This transformation is itself the fruit of Christ’s Ascension and of the Church’s participation in His Ascension, in His new life. All attempts to ‘explain’ what happens in the Eucharist in terms of ‘substance’ and ‘accidents’ (the western doctrine of transubstantiation, unfortunately taught sometimes as an Orthodox one) or in terms of time (the ‘moment of transformation’) are inadequate and futile precisely because they apply to the Eucharist the categories of ‘this world’ while its very essence is in transcending these categories, in introducing us into the dimensions and the reality of the new age. This (the transformation) happens not because of some strange and miraculous power left by Christ with some people (priests) and who therefore can perform this miracle by virtue of their power, but because we – the Church – are in Christ, i.e. in His Sacrifice, Love, Ascension, in the whole of His movement of deification, of transforming His Humanity by His Divinity; because, in other words, we are in His Eucharist and offer Him as our Eucharist to God.

St John of Damascus (657-750) commented:

“If you inquire how this happens, it is enough for you to learn that it is through the Holy Spirit… we know nothing more than this, that the Word of God is true, active, and omnipotent, but in its manner of operation, unsearchable.

The mystery of the eucharistic transformation is thus the mystery of the Church herself, of her belonging to the new age and the new life – in the Holy Spirit.

And when we do this, as He told us we should, we – the Church – are accepted where He has entered. And being accepted, we eat and drink at His Table in His Kingdom” (St Luke 22.30) as He said we would. Since the Kingdom is He Himself, the Divine Life communicated to us at this Heavenly banquet, we receive Him as the new food of our new life.

For ‘this world’ for which the Kingdom of God is yet to come, for its ‘objective categories,’ the Bread remains bread and the Wine remains wine. But in the wonderful and transfigured reality of the Kingdom, revealed and manifested in the Church they are verily and totally the very Body and the very Blood of Christ.

Theotokos

The title, Mother of God, took on a great importance in the fourth century, when a heretic named Nestorius – a man who held high office in the Church – claimed the one in Mary’s womb was certainly man, but that He was not God. Orthodox Christians, with one accord, said ‘Wrong!’ …for unless the One in Mary’s womb was and is God, we are dead in our sins. To safeguard the full deity of Christ, the Church has always insisted that Mary be rightly called – as Elizabeth discerned her to be (St Luke 1.43) – the Mother of God. This title, of course, does not mean mother of the Holy Trinity, for the Holy Trinity has no mother. Neither does it mean she originated the Person who is God the Son, for He exists before all time. It refers instead to Mary being the God-bearer (Theotokos in Greek), Mother of the eternal Son of God, who assumed full humanity in her womb.

The History of the Lord’s Prayer in the Liturgy

One of the first references to the Lord’s Prayer in the liturgy at Constantinople is the famous sermon of St John Chrysostom given in July 399 concerning the fallen Eutropius. Eutropius was born a slave, but through strange circumstances became the Consul of Emperor Arcadius. By intrigue and deception, and by surrounding the emperor with worldly pleasures, Eutropius became the real power behind the throne and perpetrated all sorts of evil. He eliminated all of his competitors and even cut off their means of escape by abolishing the Church’s right to grant asylum to fugitives – much to the dismay of Archbishop John Chrysostom. Eutropius’ fortunes dramatically reversed one day and with imperial soldiers in pursuit, he fled to the church, drew aside the altar curtains and clung to one of its columns. From there he begged Chrysostom for asylum.

St Augustine (†430) explained in his Sermo Denis 6,3, “Why is it [the Lord’s Prayer] recited before receiving the Body and Blood of Christ? Because human frailty is such that perhaps we entertained some improper thought, something came from our tongue that should not have, the eye glanced at something improper, if the ears heard some enticement they should not have, if perhaps some things have happened as a result of this world’s temptation and the weakness of human life, it is wiped clean by the Lord’s Prayer, where it is said ‘Forgive us our trespasses;’ so that we might approach [the Eucharist] safely, lest we eat and drink what we receive unto our judgment.”

The next day was Sunday and people flooded into the church to hear what was happening. As Chrysostom began his sermon the curtain of the sanctuary was drawn aside revealing the cowering form of the miserable Eutropius clinging to one of the columns of the Holy Table. The Lord’s Prayer

The Lord’s Prayer is the quintessential prayer of all Christianity, … It is the prayer from which all the other prayers of the Church are derived, either directly or indirectly, and which in itself contains the essence of the message of the faith and prescription for salvation….

Throughout the gospels Jesus Christ frequently…refers to the supra-heavenly God, God the Father, as ‘My Father’. Always, in referring to the Father, this familiar ‘My’ is used, thus identifying Himself in a fully filial relationship to the Godhead. No one anywhere else in Holy Scripture takes such liberty.

Yet in the Lord’s Prayer, He extends to us fallen creatures the incomparable and unique privilege of addressing the divine Majesty of the Lord of all, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, the Creator and Ruler of cosmos, He who is and ever shall be, as ‘Father’…. The Greek word is epouranion, supra-heavenly, to remind us that God is not of this world, nor of its families. He transcends all that we know and can conceive, and that we can approach Him only through His only-begotten Son alone. Note the words of the Liturgy which immediately precede the prayer: ‘And make us worthy O master, that with boldness and without condemnation we may dare to call on Thee, the heavenly God, as Father, and to say:’

That we may dare! Dare to call the Lord of all ‘Daddy’ even as we would address our own parent! Note that in the prayer, Jesus tells us to address God as ‘Our Father. We are never told to address Him as ‘my Father’. That degree of possessiveness is reserved entirely to the Son of God – Jesus Christ – who is His Son from all eternity by nature. We as human beings may yet become what Christ is by nature through the operation of divine grace. Thus in that sense we, too, may become children of God therefore brothers and sisters of Christ, as it were, by adoption through His infinite mercy and infinite love in the Holy Spirit. And therefore since we belong one to another, all partake equally and uniformly of our human nature. We do so collectively and never as individuals; we must address the living God as Our Father’ and never in an individual or possessive way.

The Elevation

The priest takes in his hands the Divine Bread and lifts it up saying ‘The Holy things are for the holy!’ This ancient rite of Elevation is the original form of communion. In its perfect conciseness it expresses the antinomy*, the supra-rational nature of religion. It forbids anyone who is not holy to partake of the Divine Holiness. But none is holy, but the Holy One. And the choir answers, affirming it: One is Holy. One is the Lord Jesus Christ. Yet, come and receive, for He has sanctified us with His Holiness, has made us His holy people. Again and again the mystery of the Eucharist is revealed as the mystery of the Church, the Body of Christ in which we eternally become what we are called to be.

Breaking the Bread (Fraction)

The early Church called the whole eucharistic service “breaking of bread,” for this rite was central in the liturgical celebration. Its meaning is clear: the same and one bread which is given to many, is the same and one Christ becoming the life of many, uniting them in Himself: Unite all of us to one another who become partakers of the one Bread and Cup in the Communion of the Holy Spirit …(the Liturgy of St Basil).

This is the one source of life that brings all to it and redeems the unity of all men under one Head – Christ.

“Even though every day the immaculate Body of Christ is broken and His Blood is poured out on the Holy Altar, still the smallest particle of the Lamb is the whole Christ. And even though many liturgies are performed at the same moment around the world, still there are not many bodies of Jesus Christ, since in each the same Christ is present. So when someone communes in the smallest part of the Bread, he communes in His whole Body and Blood.

Commingling

The commingling rite, i.e. placing the Holy Bread into the Chalice, is an ancient part of the Divine Liturgy. Theodore of Mopsuestia (ca. 392) writes: ‘And he [the bishop] joins them together reuniting the Body and Blood, showing everyone that, although two, they are one in power and are not separate after the separation which occurred at the moment of the death and passion undergone by the Body of our Lord when He was crucified on the cross for all of us. …for this reason it is customary to drop the Body into the Chalice little by little, in order to show that they are inseparable and one in power and confer the same grace to those who receive them.’

The commingling ‘completes’ the mystery by perfecting the union of the elements as a sign of the resurrection and consequently the union of Body, Blood, and Spirit in the incorruptible Risen Lord, so that they may return united to us in communion. Though the reality was already accomplished at the Epiclesis [the invocation of the Holy Spirit], it must also be so in sign at the completion of the sacrifice, before being broken and distributed in communion.

Whereas the consecrated Bread and Wine is a twofold sign of Christ’s sacrificial Body and Blood, the commingling, symbolizing the union of these two species, images the glorified humanity of the Risen Christ and our participation, via communion, in His risen life.

The Rite of Warmth (Zeon)16

After placing the Holy Bread into the Chalice, a little warm water is added. This rite is particular to the Byzantine Liturgy and is found in no other liturgical tradition. From the 11th century on, Byzantine commentators explained that the use of hot water was to ‘show that we receive in communion not the cold body and blood of a cadaver, but the warm and life-giving Body and Blood of our Redeemer.’

We receive in communion the warm and life-giving Body and Blood of our Redeemer.

Nicholas Cabasilas (ca. 1350) offered one explanation of the rite: The priest drops into the chalice a little warm water to symbolize the descent of the Holy Spirit upon the Church. For the Holy Spirit is given when the whole plan of redemption is completed. And now the descent of the Spirit comes about when the sacrifice has been offered and the holy offerings have reached their perfection.…. What is the effect and the result of the sufferings and works and teaching of Christ? Considered in relation to ourselves, it is nothing other than the descent of the Holy Spirit upon the Church.

So it is fitting that this should be represented after the other mysteries, as it is when the warm water is poured into the chalice. Since this warm water is not only water, but shares the nature of fire, it signifies the Holy Spirit, who is sometimes represented by water, and who came down upon the Apostles in the form of fire. …for the mysteries also represent the Church, which is the Body of Christ; she received the Holy Spirit after our Lord’s ascension; now she receives the gift of the Holy Spirit after the offerings have been accepted at the heavenly altar. God, who has accepted them, sends us the Holy Spirit in return, as we have said; for then and now there is one Mediator and one Spirit.

Giving Communion with the Spoon

We do not know precisely when and where the custom began of giving communion with a spoon. The council in Trullo of 692 forbade the use of any kind of receptacle for receiving Communion, and affirmed that the hands of a living man, made in the image and likeness of God, are the only appropriate receptacle for the sacrament of the Body of Christ. The spoon seems to have been introduced some time during the eighth century, and its use gradually spread. In its origin, it stems from a practical problem. Its primary intention was to be able to give communion to large numbers of people quickly and to make them eat the Holy Gifts. In ancient times there arose a certain problem with some not eating, but taking the Eucharist home to use it for superstitious purposes.

The Prayer Before Communion

We receive the heavenly Bread and the heavenly Drink which restores us and leads us to eternal life.

No one merits communion: no one is worthy, holy, or righteous enough by themselves to receive it. Yet this saving blessing comes to us as a free gift from God, and for this reason we refer to the sacred species — the bread and the wine which are consecrated for us — as the Holy Gifts. In the Eucharist we offer to God those very basic elements upon which our physical life depends — elements of our food and drink — and in return we receive the heavenly Bread and the heavenly Drink which restores us and leads us to eternal life.

Instead of fearing death, Christians trample on death as on something dead.

St Athanasius was born in Alexandria, Egypt, around 298 AD, only a few years before the last and greatest persecution of Christians was begun by Emperor Diocletian. From the time he was about 5 years old until he was 11, Athanasius personally witnessed many of his loved ones, neighbors, and play-mates being killed for their faith in Christ. He later described how willingly and even joyfully they gave their life for their Savior, many going to their death singing hymns of praise to God. In his work On the Incarnation he wrote: All the disciples of Christ despise death; they take the offensive against it and, instead of fearing it, by the sign of the cross and by faith in Christ, trample on it as on something dead. Before the divine sojourn of the Savior, even the holiest of men were afraid of death, and mourned the dead as those who perish. But now that the Savior has raised His Body, death is no longer terrible, but all those who believe in Christ tread it underfoot as nothing, and prefer to die rather than to deny their faith in Christ, knowing full well that when they die they do not perish, but live indeed, and become incorruptible through the resurrection…. If you see with your own eyes men and women and even children thus welcoming death for the sake of Christ’s religion, how can you be so utterly silly and incredulous and maimed in your mind as not to realize that Christ, to Whom these all bear witness, Himself gives the victory to each, making death completely powerless for those who hold his faith and bear the sign of the cross?

That All Day Long We May Meditate Upon Thy Righteousness

The past, the present and the future have been given by Christ a new and joyful meaning.

The reality of the Eucharist celebration does not end with the closing of the liturgical service. It can transform every moment of our lives if all day long we meditate on and remember God, His righteousness, and His love for us in Christ. A Christian is the one who, first of all, remembers. He can forget Christ; he can wake up in the morning and think only of the petty concerns of that particular day. Yet, on a deeper level, even these minor concerns become a very different experience if he remembers that he is not simply John Smith who has to do this or that, but the one to whom Christ has come, whose life Christ has assumed and has given a new meaning…. It is a time radically different from that of a person whose only concern in life is to mow his lawn, to have money in the bank, and then to trade in his Chevy for a Cadillac and the Cadillac for something else, and finally to die saying, ‘Well, I had all this and nothing has a meaning.’ It is sad, it is tragically sad, not to know that the past, the present, and the future have been given by Christ a new and joyful meaning.
